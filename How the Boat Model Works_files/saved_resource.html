How the Boat Model Works

A build explained from zero

How the boat model works

A 10-metre robot boat, a rough sea, and roughly two thousand lines of Python that predict what happens when they meet. No fluid mechanics assumed — every term is defined where it first appears.

This describes a working simulator, part by part. The goal is not to list the theories it uses but to say what each piece actually computes: what numbers go in, what comes out, and why that step has to exist at all.

The boat is 10 m long, 2.5 m wide, floats 0.8 m deep, and weighs about 9.1 tonnes. It has no crew. It operates in open water where there is nothing to collide with, so the only thing that can hurt it is the sea itself.

That split is the whole design. Solving how water flows around a hull is expensive. Doing it once, storing the answer, and turning that answer into something a computer can evaluate in microseconds is what makes the rest possible.

Stage 1 · the input

What a sea actually is, as a number

Start with one wave. A wave on deep water is not water moving along — the water mostly circles in place, and what travels is the shape. Two things govern it: gravity pulls the crests down, and the water's inertia makes them overshoot. That balance sets a rigid relationship between a wave's length and its period:

wavelength ≈ 1.56 × (period)²  metres, seconds

a 10-second wave is 156 m long; a 4-second wave is 25 m long
Dispersion

The rule above. Its consequence is that long waves travel faster than short ones. That is why a distant storm sends smooth long swell first and choppy short waves later — the sea sorts them by speed on the way over.

A real sea is not one wave. It is thousands, of different lengths, running in slightly different directions, adding up. Where crests coincide you get a big one; where a crest meets a trough they cancel. The sum looks random, and it is — but its statistics are stable and measurable.

Wave spectrum

A recipe that says how much energy the sea contains at each wave period. Feed it random phases and you get a specific sea surface; feed it different random phases and you get a different-looking sea with the same character. This model uses the JONSWAP spectrum, fitted from real North Sea measurements in the 1970s.

Two numbers summarise a spectrum, and they are what a weather forecast gives you:

Significant wave height Hs Roughly the average height of the biggest third of waves — and close to what an experienced observer would call "the wave height". Here 3.25 m.
Peak period Tp The period carrying the most energy. Here 9.7 s, so the dominant waves are about 147 m long.

That combination is Sea State 5: rough, whitecaps everywhere, the upper end of what a small unmanned boat would be sent out in. Sea State 4 is a step below.

What the code stores

The continuous spectrum is chopped into a finite list — typically 16 periods × 4 directions = 64 individual waves. Each one carries five numbers: height, wavelength, direction, period, and a random starting phase. The sea surface at any point and any instant is then just a sum:

height(x, y, t) = Σ aj · cos( how far along this wave you are − how much time has passed + random offset )

This is the single most important practical property of the whole model: the sea is a formula, not a recording. You can ask for the water height at any position, at any time, past or future, instantly. Nothing is stored. The visualiser in the browser recomputes the same 64 waves on the GPU sixty times a second, which is why the water there is not a video — it is the same sea the physics used, evaluated independently.

Why this matters later

Because the future sea is computable, the simulator can hand the controller a perfect forecast. That lets us measure the maximum possible value of a wave sensor before anyone buys one — an ideal sensor with zero error. If perfect information is worth nothing, no real sensor can be worth more.

Stage 2 · the object

Why a boat in waves is a hard problem

A floating body can move six independent ways, and all six matter:

MotionPlain meaningWhy it matters here
Surgeforward and backthis is speed — the only thing the propeller controls
Swaysideways driftpushes the boat off its track
Heaveup and downthe main response to waves this size
Rolltipping side to sidecapsize risk in beam seas
Pitchbow up, bow downcombined with heave, decides whether the bow slams
Yawturning left and rightheading — the rudder's job

If water were empty space this would be easy: six equations, done. Water complicates it in three specific ways, and each one costs a piece of machinery further down.

1. The boat has to drag water with it

Added mass

To accelerate, the hull must also accelerate the water around it. The extra force needed is proportional to acceleration — exactly what mass does — so it behaves like the boat being heavier than it is. For heave on this hull, the added mass is about 8.5 tonnes on top of 9.1: the boat is nearly twice as heavy vertically as it is on a crane.

Awkwardly, it is not a constant. How much water comes along depends on how fast you are oscillating, because the water surface has time to respond differently at different frequencies.

2. Moving makes waves, and waves cost energy

Radiation damping

A hull bobbing up and down sends out ripples. Those ripples carry energy away, and the energy comes from the boat's motion — so the water pushes back with a force proportional to velocity, which is what a damper does. This is friction-free damping: it exists even in a perfectly slippery fluid, purely because energy leaves as waves.

3. The waves you made come back

This is the one that shapes the entire architecture. The ripples the hull radiated a moment ago are still out there, and they are still pressing on the hull. The force on the boat right now depends on what the boat was doing seconds ago.

Fluid memory

The hull's past motion keeps influencing its present forces. For this boat the memory lasts 2.2 to 2.9 seconds — against a wave period of about 8 seconds, that is a quarter of the forcing cycle. It is not a detail that can be ignored.

This is the dividing line

Marine simulators built on game engines — and most published robot-boat research — leave memory out. They compute buoyancy from the instantaneous water surface and treat waves as a disturbance with no history. That is a defensible model for steering around obstacles in mild water. It is the wrong model for a boat whose job is to survive a rough sea, and it is the reason this project builds its own simulator instead of using an existing one.

Natural periods — and the ratio that explains everything

Push a floating boat down and let go, and it bobs at its own natural rhythm, set by its mass against the stiffness of buoyancy. Measured from the model:

Heave2.04 s per bob
Pitch1.76 s
Roll1.49 s
Waves push at7.48 s as felt underway

The sea pushes about four times slower than the boat naturally wants to move. That is a long way from resonance, and it puts the boat in a distinct regime:

Contouring

When the waves are much longer than the boat and much slower than its natural rhythm, the boat simply rides the surface like a cork — up the slope, over the crest, down the far side. It follows the water rather than fighting it. Here the dominant wave is 147 m against a 10 m hull, a ratio of about 15.

Contouring has consequences that turn up repeatedly later. The boat's vertical motion is almost entirely dictated by the wave shape, not by the boat's own dynamics. Damping barely matters, because damping only matters near resonance. And the bow rarely leaves the water, which makes slamming a rare event and therefore a bad thing to measure.

Stage 3 · the expensive part, done once

Solving the water

We need three families of force: what the passing wave does to a stationary hull, and what the hull's own motion does to itself. Getting them means solving for the flow around the hull — the expensive step in the pipeline.

Potential flow

A simplification that makes the problem solvable: assume the water has no viscosity — no stickiness, no friction, no turbulence. Under that assumption the entire flow can be described by a single scalar field, and — crucially — the equations only need to be solved on the surfaces, not throughout the volume of water. A three-dimensional problem collapses to a two-dimensional one.

What it throws away: friction drag, flow separation, breaking waves, and almost all damping of roll. Those come back later as separate, measured terms.

Boundary element method (BEM)

The numerical technique. The hull is tiled with small flat panels — 1056 of them here. Each panel is treated as a tiny source that pushes water outward, and the computer solves for how strong each source must be so that, added together, no water flows through the hull anywhere. Once the strengths are known, the pressure anywhere follows.

This is run at 150 different wave frequencies, from very long swell to short chop. Each run yields a set of matrices describing forces, and the forces split cleanly into three physically distinct parts:

ComponentWhat it isPicture it as
Froude–Krylovthe pressure the wave would have had at that spot if the boat were not therethe sea pushing on a boat-shaped hole in the water
Diffractionthe correction because the boat is there and scatters the wavethe wave bending around an obstacle
Radiationthe force from the boat's own motion — added mass and dampingthe ripples the boat makes, pushing back

How we know it is right

A solver like this can be confidently wrong, so it is checked against a shape with a known exact answer — a floating sphere, worked out analytically in 1982. Two independent limits plus one internal symmetry law:

TestComputedShould be
Sphere added mass, very long waves0.84790.831
Sphere added mass, very short waves0.49950.5
Reciprocity (a symmetry the physics demands)0.000690
The first version of this test failed, and the test was wrong

It reported 0.912 against 0.831. The solver was fine; the check extrapolated to "very long waves" by drawing a straight line through data points that sat on the far side of a peak in the curve. Spacing the sample frequencies logarithmically fixed it. A second independent limit was then added, because one test cannot tell a correct answer from a lucky one.

Stage 4 · the translation

Turning a frequency answer into a time answer

The solver gives answers per frequency: "if the boat heaves up and down forever at exactly this rate, here is the force." That is a useful answer and a useless one. A real boat under control never does anything forever at one rate — it speeds up, it gets hit by an odd wave, the propeller changes. We need a model that works instant by instant.

The bridge is a result from 1962 that says: take the added mass at infinitely fast oscillation (a single constant), and account for everything else — all the frequency-dependence — as memory:

(mass + constant added mass) × acceleration
  + everything the hull did in the last 3 seconds, weighted
  + buoyancy pulling it back level
= force from the waves
Memory kernel

The weighting function in that middle line. It answers: "if the hull twitched once, how much force does that twitch still exert t seconds later?" It starts large and decays to nothing after 2.2–2.9 s. Every frequency-dependent effect from Stage 3 is packed into this one curve.

A sign error that passed its own test

The formula converting frequency data into the memory kernel was implemented with one term's sign flipped. The symptom was distinctive: a 200% error that was identical across four unrelated motions — the signature of a convention mistake, not a numerical one.

The unit test could not catch it, because the test generated its own reference data using the same flipped convention. The round trip closed perfectly. A test that checks a computation against itself cannot detect a wrong convention — only an outside source of truth can. This is the single most transferable lesson in the project.

Making the memory cheap

Evaluating "everything from the last 3 seconds" at every timestep means storing history and integrating over it — slow, and awkward for a controller. So the memory curve is replaced by something equivalent and much faster.

State-space realisation

Instead of remembering the past, add a handful of extra internal variables that carry the memory forward automatically — the way a swinging pendulum "remembers" the push that started it without storing a log. The algorithm that finds them (ERA, from 1985) arranges samples of the memory curve into a large matrix, factorises it, and reads the internal variables off the dominant part.

Two things learned the hard way here. The matrix has to be sized in seconds, not in data points — sized in points it covered only 0.94 s of a 2.9 s curve. And asking for exactly the right number of internal variables gave a fit eight times worse than asking for too many and then discarding the ones that decayed too slowly to be real.

ResultA boat described by about 110 numbers, updated by ordinary arithmetic
Speed29× faster than real time — 30 minutes of sea in one minute
AccuracyMotion matches the exact frequency answer to 0.1–0.3%

Stage 5 · putting the physics back

What the clean model misses

Everything so far assumes small motions: the waterline stays where it was drawn, forces scale in proportion, doubling the wave doubles the response. In Sea State 5 the waves are four times the boat's draft. None of that holds.

Slicing the hull

Stations

The hull is cut into 41 cross-sections along its length, like slices of bread. At each instant the code works out where the actual water surface sits on each slice, and how much of that slice is underwater right now. Buoyancy is then summed over the slices — genuinely, from the real waterline, not from an assumed one.

For a hull given by a formula this is exact algebra. For an arbitrary 3-D scanned hull the code intersects the mesh with each slicing plane and integrates the resulting polygon exactly. That path had two bugs worth naming: an early approximation biased the volume +8%, and the exact version then read −3.6% and refused to converge — because a slicing plane landing exactly on a line of mesh vertices found nothing to intersect. Nudging each plane by a millionth of the hull length brought the error to +0.018%.

Adding corrections without breaking what already works

Slice-based theory is cruder than the full 3-D solver, so replacing the good forces with it would be a step backwards. Instead only the difference is added:

force = accurate 3-D linear force + ( slice force with real waterline − slice force with flat waterline )

In small waves the bracket cancels to zero and the model is exactly as accurate as Stage 4. In big waves it supplies the nonlinear part. Measured error in the small-wave limit: 0.21%.

The other terms

Pressure fades with depth

A wave's push weakens exponentially below the surface, so a hull 0.8 m down feels less than the surface height suggests. This was once applied at 1.5× the correct depth. The giveaway: the error grew with wave frequency, from 2.2% to 21.4% — short waves fade fastest, so a depth mistake shows up there first.

Added resistance

A boat pitching in waves radiates energy, and that energy is paid for by slowing down. It is why a boat that does 8 knots in calm water might manage 5 in a rough sea, with the throttle unchanged.

Slamming

The bow lifts clear of the water, then crashes back in. Two things happen: a brief, violent force, and the water suddenly having to be accelerated out of the way.

The model computes this as the added mass changing while the bow enters. That term needs no threshold and no "slam detector": below the keel the wetted width is zero so the force is zero, at the design waterline the section is at its widest so the width is not changing and the force is zero again. It is non-zero only in between — which is water entry. Peak force measured: 42 kN typical, 71 kN worst, against a boat that weighs 89 kN. Roughly half the boat's weight, delivered in a fraction of a second.

A big force that changes almost nothing

Feeding that impact back into the motion moves heave by 0%, pitch by 3%, and actually reduces bow acceleration by 9%. The force is huge but brief, so the momentum it transfers is small against 9 tonnes — and because it always opposes entry, it works as a cushion rather than a hammer. A useful reminder that force and effect are different questions.

Viscous damping

All the friction and swirling that potential flow deliberately ignored, added back as six coefficients. These are honest placeholders — the right shape, no measurement behind them. Section 8 shows which of them actually matter.

The machinery

The propeller is not an ideal force source: 12 kN maximum, a 1.5 s lag before thrust follows the command, a limit on how fast thrust can change, a 0.15 s transport delay, and a loss of thrust when it comes near the surface and starts sucking air. The rudder has a maximum angle, a maximum turn rate, and stalls past 28°.

Stage 6 · the decision

The controller

The boat must choose two things, continuously: how much thrust, and how much rudder.

Model predictive control (MPC)

Instead of reacting to error, the controller simulates the future. It imagines several possible sequences of throttle settings, plays each one forward, scores the outcomes, and picks the best. Then it does the whole thing again a fraction of a second later. Like a chess player looking ahead rather than a thermostat reacting.

That requires simulating the boat many times per decision, and the 110-number model is far too slow for that. So the controller carries a deliberately crude internal model.

Reduced model

A 9-number caricature of the boat, with the important behaviours and none of the detail. It is not hand-written — it is identified from the full simulator: give the detailed model a step of thrust and watch how it accelerates, push it down and watch it bob, kick the rudder and watch it turn, then fit simple curves to what happened.

The key number that comes out: the boat takes 6.3 seconds to reach a new speed after the throttle changes. Remember that one.

How the search runs

Guesses per decision192 random throttle plans
Look-ahead12 seconds, in 24 steps
Described by7 control points, smoothly joined — not 24 free values
Combiningweighted blend favouring good plans, not just the best one

Using 7 control points rather than 24 independent values matters: it makes the search a 7-dimensional problem instead of a 24-dimensional one, and it produces smooth throttle changes instead of jitter.

Steering is a separate job

Letting the same controller steer produced 476° of heading error — the boat spinning. The cause was subtle and worth stating: the rudder makes the boat slide sideways as well as turn, and the 9-number model has no sideways-slide variable, so the controller could not see the drift it was causing.

The fix was to split the jobs: MPC handles thrust, a classical autopilot handles heading. Hand-picked autopilot gains were about 15× too strong; computing them properly from the boat's measured turning response gives heading held to 0.7–0.9° and cross-track error of 1–3 m.

Tuning the priorities

The controller's score function weighs seven competing concerns — ride quality, speed, slamming, tracking, effort. Those seven weights are tuned automatically.

CMA-ES

An optimiser for problems with no gradient: try a cloud of candidate settings, keep the better half, move the cloud towards them and reshape it, repeat. Good for a handful of parameters and a noisy score.

The choice of seven tunable numbers rather than a neural network is deliberate and is the project's answer to the sim-to-real problem. Tuning seven numbers takes a few hundred trial runs — a budget that fits inside a real sea-trial campaign, so the identical procedure can be repeated on the actual boat. A policy network needing ten million trials cannot be retuned on the water, and would have to live with whatever mismatch remained between simulation and reality.

What the optimiser found is more interesting than the score: it drove the "hold your speed" weight to its minimum and the "don't change the throttle abruptly" weight to zero. It discovered on its own that in a rough sea, insisting on speed is not worth it, and that penalising throttle changes just forbids the boat from reacting.

Stage 7 · the ruler

Measuring "how bad was that"

Every quantity anyone cares about traces back to one signal: how far the water surface is from the bow, moment to moment.

Relative vertical motion

Not how much the boat moved, and not how big the wave was — the difference. If the boat rises exactly with the wave, the relative motion is zero and nothing dramatic happens. Trouble comes from the gap between them. Measured here: about 0.29 m of variation.

CriterionCondition on that one signal
Slammingbow clears the water (gap exceeds the 0.8 m draft) and re-enters fast
Deck wetnessgap exceeds the 0.55 m freeboard — water comes over the bow
Added resistancethe relative speed, squared
Bow accelerationhow sharply the gap changes — what shakes the equipment
The most expensive mistake in the project

Slamming was chosen as the headline measure. It happens about 0.3 times a minute, so a 200-second test contains one or two. Comparing two such counts carries about 59% noise — and the effect being reported was 30–47%. The result was roughly one standard deviation of pure randomness, and it did not survive a careful re-run.

The fix is not more test runs. It is to stop counting. For a threshold like this, the rate can be calculated from the statistics of the continuous signal rather than by tallying events — a 1964 result. Same definition, same physics, but the inputs are averages over thousands of samples instead of a count of two. Verified against the tally: matches within 17%, with five times less scatter.

The broader lesson generalises past boats. Ranked by how reproducible they are, the available measures span a factor of a hundred — and the one originally chosen was the worst of them:

Stage 8 · seeing it

The boat's own waves

Two separate wave systems come off a moving hull, and they differ by a factor of ten.

The unsteady one

Ripples from the hull rocking in the waves, plus the incoming wave scattering off it. These come from the same Stage-3 solution, evaluated at points on the water instead of on the hull. Measured: the hull disturbs the surface by 0.2% of the wave height — 4 centimetres against waves running ±2.2 m.

That small number settles a design question. If a forward-looking sensor sees water the boat has already disturbed, its reading is contaminated. At 0.2%, it is not.

The steady one — the wake

The V-shaped wake behind a boat was missing entirely, because the fluid solver assumes the boat is not moving forward. It had to be built separately, and it is roughly ten times larger than the ripples above — it is what you actually see from a bridge.

Its geometry follows from one requirement: a wave that stays with the boat must travel exactly as fast as the boat, in its own direction. Combined with the dispersion rule from Stage 1, that pins everything — including a fact that surprises people:

The 19.47° wedge

The V behind every displacement vessel opens at the same angle — 19°28′, which is arcsin(⅓) — whether it is a duck or a supertanker. Speed changes how far apart the crests are, never the angle. The model reproduces it at 20.8–22.3° from the simulated surface, and the crest spacing matches theory exactly at every speed tested.

In deep water the entire wake lies behind that wedge, which is a second reason a forward-looking sensor is unaffected by the boat's own waves.

For display it is precomputed into an image and looked up on the GPU, stretched by speed — the pattern is exactly self-similar in distance divided by speed squared, so one image serves every speed.

Stage 9 · what it was for

The question, and the answer

The whole build existed to answer one procurement question: how far ahead does the boat need to see the waves? A short mast sees a little way. A tall mast sees further. A tethered drone sees a long way and costs a great deal more.

The simulator can answer this with an ideal sensor — perfect knowledge of the future sea, since the sea is a formula. If perfect information is worth nothing, no purchasable sensor can be worth more.

Tested across nine different measures of "how bad was that", three strategies, ten random seas each, always compared against a plain fixed-throttle boat travelling at the same average speed:

Measures improved beyond noise0 out of 27
Look-ahead tested0 to 90 seconds

Why — and it is not about the sensor

Two numbers from earlier in this document explain it completely:

the boat takes 6.3 s to change speed
a wave arrives every 7.5 s

To do anything about a particular wave, the boat would have to act within one — so it needs to respond in well under 2 seconds. Its only actuator is three and a half times too slow. Two seconds of warning and twelve seconds of warning gave identical results, not because two was enough, but because neither was usable.

The constraint was never the sensor

Buying a drone to see further would have bought nothing. The boat cannot act on what it sees. The hardware question is not "how far ahead" but "what can move fast enough" — trim tabs or interceptors, small movable plates that generate lift in a fraction of a second, rather than a propeller that takes six seconds to change the speed of nine tonnes.

Two follow-up questions, also answered no

Could it work on a slower rhythm? Waves arrive in groups, and a group lasts 50–100 seconds — the propeller is easily fast enough for that. Tested with 90 seconds of look-ahead: still nothing, and marginally worse.

The simulation tells the controller the sea conditions — would a sensor that measures them help? A sharper question, since choosing a sensible average speed is the one thing that works. Two findings:

  • The boat already measures the sea, using itself. Because it contours, its own bobbing tracks the wave height to within 6–11%, and the dominant wave period can be read out of its motion to within 2%. No forward-looking sensor required.
  • And knowing is worth 0.7%. The best cruising speed barely changes from Sea State 4 to Sea State 5, so there is very little to adapt to.

What the project actually produced

A verified simulator — accurate to a fraction of a percent against exact solutions where exact solutions exist — and three clear negative answers that between them save an expensive sensor programme and redirect it at the actuators instead. A negative result with an error bar and a mechanism is worth considerably more than a positive one without either.

Along the way, twenty-three real defects were found and fixed. About a third of them were not modelling errors at all but measurement errors: a check that measured a smoothing window instead of the model, a convergence test that tracked a single pixel, a frequency analysis that could only return certain fixed answers, and finally a headline result resting on a count of two events. The model was usually right before the ruler was.
