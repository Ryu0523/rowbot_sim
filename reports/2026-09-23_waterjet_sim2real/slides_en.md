## Slide 2 — What changes with a waterjet

**Title:** A waterjet steers with its flow  **Figure:** `fig1_waterjet_model.png`

**How the model works**
- **Thrust.** Engine power sets how fast the pump throws water out. Thrust is the mass flow times the jet speed minus the inlet speed, so the same power gives less thrust as the boat speeds up.
- **Steering.** The nozzle turns the whole jet sideways. With no flow there is no steering: zero throttle means no steering at all. At low throttle the steering force can exceed the thrust.
- **Loss of prime.** The pump draws air when the intake leaves the water, and thrust and steering vanish together. In the full simulation this happens about 5% of the time in head seas.
- **Reverse bucket.** It turns the jet forward for braking, at about 60% of ahead thrust (manufacturer data).

**What the controller side got**
- The MPC's internal model now has steering force ∝ thrust × sin(nozzle angle), with its coefficient identified from the plant.
- The heading autopilot's gains are scheduled on thrust.

**Checks**
- The jet hull passes the project's 8-item acceptance battery. The old propeller boat's results are bit-identical.
- One hidden placeholder showed up. A jet can push the stern sideways at zero speed, and the hull spun at 78°/s because its yaw damping at high turn rates was about 20× too low. That damping now comes from a crossflow-drag estimate.

## Slide 3 — Validation against public data

**Title:** The propulsion model matches public measurements  **Figure:** `fig2_validation.png`

- **Manufacturer thrust curves** (HamiltonJet, one unit, 8 power levels). Two unknowns are fitted on one curve and the other seven are predicted, with about 2% error. Static thrust grows with power the way momentum theory says.
- **Planing resistance** (Savitsky 1964). Our code matches a published worked example to about 2%.
- **VM 18's near-twin, the Scarab 195.** Measured top speed is 42–43 kn. Jet model + Savitsky + air drag gives about 42 kn, with the unknown hull numbers sampled over realistic ranges.
- **Applied to VM 18.**
  - Predicted top speed is about 44–48 kn, so the brochure's "50+ kn" looks optimistic.
  - "26 h at 20 kn" would need roughly 500+ L of fuel, more than the dry-to-wet weight difference allows. This is worth confirming with the builder.
- **Not validated yet:** motions in waves (the VM 18 planing model does not exist yet), and jet steering force and loss of prime (no public measurements).

**Sources:** HamiltonJet unit "291" datasheet in MacPherson (SNAME NE 2000); Alourdas (2016) Savitsky example; Boating magazine tests of the Scarab 195 ID (2019, 2024).

## Slide 4 — First sim-to-real experiment

**Title:** Tune cheaply, adapt with few "real" episodes  **Figure:** `fig3_sim2real_results.png`

**Setup**
- **Low-fi world:** the MPC's own simplified model, run as the boat. It is fast.
- **"Real boat":** the full simulation with the waterjet. Its episodes are the scarce resource: 32 for adaptation.
- **Tuned:** 7 MPC cost weights plus a minimum-thrust setting.
- **Sea:** sea state 5, head and 45° bow seas.
- **Evaluation:** every method runs on the same 16 unseen sea realizations (paired comparison).

**Results**
- Tuning only in the low-fi world is no better than hand-tuning.
- Randomizing the low-fi world's parameters is unreliable: +29%, then +7% with another seed.
- With 32 target episodes:
  - an evolution strategy gains nothing significant;
  - a Gaussian-process search gains +13%, then 0%;
  - the same search with the low-fi world as a prior gains +25% and +34%. It is the only method that helped in both repeats.
- A large-budget reference with 320 target episodes gains +40%.

**Speaker note:** Two repeats per method is preliminary. The ranking between methods is the message, not the exact percentages.

## Slide 5 — Why the low-fi world failed, and what's next

**Title:** Sim-to-real only transfers what the low-fi world contains  **Figure:** `fig4_why_lowfi_fails.png`

**Findings**
- **The low-fi world cannot rank controllers.** Over 13 controllers, its ranking is unrelated to the full simulation's (slightly negative).
- **The best strategy in the full simulation is to slow down in big seas.** Tuned controllers get there through the cross-track weight: waves push the heading off, the MPC predicts growing cross-track error, and it slows down.
- **In the low-fi world that weight does nothing.** It has no lateral wave force and no added resistance, so its score does not change at all when the weight changes.
- **With no wave preview, the MPC's wave model and motion weights never affect its decisions.** That is why adapting the MPC's internal model did nothing. Parameters worth adapting must be ones the decisions depend on.

**Next steps**
1. Give the low-fi model the missing mechanisms as tunable terms (added resistance, lateral wave force), and make the speed set-point a sea-state-dependent parameter. This is "context → MPC parameters".
2. Turn on wave preview so that the wave terms matter, then re-test model adaptation.
3. Run more seeds; repeat on a public benchmark (safe-control-gym).
4. Build the VM 18 planing model once hull data arrive.

**Limits:**
- stand-in hull;
- one sea state;
- 2 target episodes per candidate;
- 1–2 repeats per method;
- the score barely penalizes slowing down, so "slow down" wins. A speed-critical mission would change this.
